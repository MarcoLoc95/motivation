It started with a course evaluation

Earlier this year, a student wrote in my course evaluation that classmates had pasted our programming project into an AI tool and then forged their AI statements.

To be honest, I was quite irritated. After all the effort I had put into the course, they simply decided to skip the learning? Then I changed perspective, and suddenly I got curious. They had the most powerful learning tool they had ever used, and I realised that nobody had shown them the other way.

Meet Lazy Susan and Sue Doku

Lazy Susanuses AI as a shortcut
  1. Pastes the assignment into AI
  2. Hands in the answer
Sue Dokuuses AI as a tutor
  1. Tries it alone first
  2. Asks AI to challenge the idea
  3. Explains it in their own words

What’s left of it?

  • Hand-in day: both hand in a good answer and get a good grade.
  • A week later: Susan can’t explain how it works; Sue explains it to a classmate.
  • Exam day, no AI: Susan is stuck on a similar problem; Sue solves a new variation.

Sue Doku’s path is what I design for: open questions without a single right answer, live presentations where you explain your own work, and honest conversations about where AI helps you think, and where, instead, it starts thinking for you.

Why this role, and why Deloitte

From your vacancy, I see you’re looking for someone who connects strategy with execution and measures what changes. That is the part of my current job I like most. I start from what people need to be able to do, build the learning around it, listen to the learners, and check whether anything actually changed. If it didn’t, I change the design.

Your consultants face the same question as my students, just with higher stakes: how do I use AI well in my own work, starting tomorrow? That is the job I want: helping thousands of professionals answer that question. And I’m looking for variety. At university, the risk is settling into a fixed set of courses and teaching the same thing for years. At Deloitte, every team and every new AI development would bring a different challenge.

Why now

I love teaching, but I no longer do research, and in Dutch academia that makes a teaching career temporary by default. I want to keep doing what I’m good at, with people who use what they learn the next day, somewhere I can grow in the long term rather than from contract to contract.

What I want to keep

Above all, I want to keep my autonomy: deciding myself which format works best for a topic, and having the trust to try different things and refine them as I go. I also want to keep the human contact with learners, whether in a workshop or one on one, and flexible hours: choosing my own schedule, as long as the work is done on time.

I’d also like to be open about one thing: I’m training as a coach and counsellor, and I’d like to practise one day a week as a freelancer (ZZP). Your vacancy offers a 32-hour week, which fits perfectly, and coaching is a skill I would bring straight into the role.
